Lesson 77 — Career: portfolio, interviews & growth

Skill gets you capable; evidence, communication and consistency get you hired and promoted. This lesson turns what you've built into a career: a portfolio that persuades, interview preparation, and habits for continued growth.

Your portfolio: evidence, not a list of tutorials

  • 3–5 strong projects beat 15 weak ones. Use Projects 3, 5 and 6 plus the capstone. Each should be deployed, fast, accessible and polished.
  • For each project, write a short case study: the problem, your role, key decisions and trade-offs, a hard problem you solved (with a before/after metric), tech stack, what you'd improve, links to live demo + code.
  • Show the engineering: tests, CI badge, architecture diagram, performance numbers, accessibility audit, clean README. Reviewers open the repo.
  • Include a 3D/interactive showpiece if you're targeting creative/3D roles, but make sure the site itself loads fast and works without it. Don't let flashiness replace usability.
  • Your site must be itself an example of mastery: semantic HTML, responsive, keyboard-accessible, Lighthouse green, no layout shift, a clear "about + contact + resume" path within 10 seconds.

Resume, GitHub and online presence

  • One page. Lead with impact: "Reduced LCP from 4.1 s to 1.3 s by …", "Built X used by N people". Avoid skill-bar graphics and lists of every tool you touched once.
  • Pin your best repos with real READMEs; keep commit history meaningful. Contributions to open source (even docs and small bugs) signal collaboration ability.
  • Write: short posts about things you learned (debugging stories, performance wins) show communication and depth. Share demos with short videos/GIFs.

Classic front-end exercises to be able to write cold

During the interview

  • Clarify first, state your approach, then code. Narrate trade-offs. Start with a working simple solution, then optimize.
  • Handle edge cases explicitly (empty, null, duplicates, large input). Run through an example by hand. Name things well even under time pressure.
  • If stuck, say what you've tried and ask a focused question. Interviewers hire people they'd enjoy working with.
  • Ask good questions at the end: how the team ships and reviews code, what the on-call looks like, how they measure success, what the first 90 days look like.

Finding and evaluating roles

  • Roles to target: front-end engineer, full-stack engineer, UI/design engineer, creative developer / WebGL engineer, developer experience, technical artist. Read job posts to see recurring skills and fill gaps with projects.
  • Channels: referrals (the best), company career pages, communities (local meetups, Discords), open-source involvement, direct outreach with a specific note showing you used their product.
  • Evaluate teams: mentorship, code review culture, test/CI health, how decisions are made, turnover, growth paths, compensation transparency. Negotiate: research ranges, anchor reasonably, consider total compensation and growth, get the offer in writing.

Growing after the first job

  • Depth + breadth ("T-shaped"): go deep in one area (performance, graphics, accessibility, design systems, backend) while staying useful across the stack.
  • Scope growth: junior (tasks with guidance) → mid (features independently) → senior (ambiguous problems, mentoring, cross-team impact) → staff+ (org-level technical direction). Impact, not years, is the currency.
  • Habits: read other people's code, review PRs thoughtfully, write postmortems and design docs, keep a "brag document" of accomplishments, ask for feedback regularly, teach others.
  • Learning loop: build small projects that stretch you; read specs and source code (Lesson 78); follow a few high-quality sources (web.dev, MDN, React blog, Three.js forum, Frontend Masters, conference talks) instead of everything.
  • Sustainability: avoid burnout, protect focus time, step away, maintain a life outside code. Careers are long. Impostor feelings are normal; keep a record of what you've shipped.

Freelancing and building products

  • Narrow positioning beats "I do websites": e.g., "interactive 3D product pages for DTC brands". Show results, price on value, use contracts and milestones, communicate often.
  • Your own product: validate before building (talk to users, landing page, pre-sales), ship small, measure, iterate. Handle payments, legal basics, privacy and support from the start.

Exercises

  1. Write a case study for your best project following the structure above, including at least two measured improvements, and publish it on your portfolio site.
  2. Audit your portfolio site: Lighthouse ≥ 95 across the board, axe clean, keyboard-only tour, test on a phone, a 10-second skim test with a friend. Fix everything found.
  3. Write 8 STAR stories (conflict, failure, leadership, ambiguity, deadline, learning, disagreement with a decision, biggest achievement) with concrete numbers.
  4. Implement from scratch, under timer: debounce, throttle, Promise.all, deep clone, EventEmitter, curry, an autocomplete, a modal with focus trap, and star rating. Record yourself narrating one and critique it.
  5. Do three mock interviews (coding, front-end design, behavioral) with peers or a platform; keep a log of feedback and improvements.
  6. Draft a 12-month growth plan: target role, three skills to deepen, one open-source contribution goal, and one public artifact (post/talk) per quarter.

Checkpoint

What makes a portfolio project persuasive?It's live, polished and fast, solves a real problem, and the case study shows decisions, trade-offs and measured results, with a repo that has tests, CI and a clear README.
What should you do when stuck in a coding interview?Think aloud, restate the problem, try a small example, offer a brute-force solution, then ask a focused question. Communication matters as much as the final answer.
